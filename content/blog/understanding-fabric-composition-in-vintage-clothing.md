**Short answer:** Knowing fabric composition is essential for assessing the quality, comfort, and longevity of a vintage piece, often more so than brand name or print, because composition determines how the garment ages and how it needs to be cared for.

Vintage shopping is visual by nature: a great print, an interesting silhouette, or an unusual color is usually what stops a scroll. Fabric composition rarely gets the same attention, even though it's the single biggest factor in whether a piece holds up for another decade or falls apart after a few washes. A buyer who checks composition before purchasing is making a meaningfully more informed decision than one going on photos alone.

## Common vintage fabrics and what they tell you

| Fabric | Feel and characteristics | Care needs |
|---|---|---|
| Wool | Warm, structured, resists wrinkling, ages well if stored properly | Dry clean or hand wash cold, store with cedar to deter moths |
| Cotton | Breathable, softens with age, can shrink if not pre-treated | Machine washable, check for shrinkage on first wash |
| Silk | Lightweight, drapes well, prone to water spotting and sun fading | Dry clean or gentle hand wash, avoid direct sunlight storage |
| Linen | Durable, breathable, wrinkles easily, improves with wear | Machine washable, iron while damp |
| Rayon | Soft drape similar to silk, but weakens significantly with age and moisture | Dry clean recommended, handle gently, check for fiber weakness before buying |
| Polyester blends | Resists wrinkling, can pill over time, doesn't breathe like natural fiber | Machine washable, low maintenance but variable longevity |

## Assessing fabric quality without touching the garment

Online vintage shopping means you usually can't feel the fabric before buying, so the evaluation shifts to other signals. Check listing photos for how the fabric drapes, whether it catches light in a way that suggests a tight weave, and whether there's visible pilling, fraying, or sheen loss. Ask sellers directly about weight and hand-feel if it's not described. Listings that include a composition tag photo are a strong signal of a seller who knows what they're selling.

## Why fiber content changes how long a garment lasts

Natural fibers like wool and cotton tend to be more durable over decades than many synthetic alternatives, particularly rayon, which is prone to weakening with age and exposure to moisture. That's part of why so much surviving vintage clothing skews toward wool coats, cotton workwear, and denim: those fibers simply hold up. A polyester blend from the 80s might look pristine in photos but feel stiff or papery in hand because synthetic fibers degrade differently than natural ones.

## Blends versus pure materials

A garment tagged "70% wool, 30% nylon" behaves differently than 100% wool: the nylon adds durability and reduces wrinkling but changes the drape and breathability. Blends aren't inherently worse, they're a different trade-off. The key is knowing what trade-off you're buying. A pure silk blouse will need more careful handling than a silk-poly blend, but it will also drape and feel noticeably different. Read the full composition line, not just the dominant fiber, since a "wool" coat that's actually 60% wool and 40% acrylic will wear and feel meaningfully different from one that's pure wool.

Fabric tags only tell part of the story on materials that get faked often.
