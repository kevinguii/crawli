**Short answer:** When identical photos of a high-demand vintage item appear on two or more secondhand platforms from different usernames, it's almost always a fraud where the scammer uses stolen images to collect payments without shipping. Cross-platform searching immediately reveals the duplication and protects you before you pay.

Image theft is the most common scam on secondhand fashion platforms. A scammer finds a real listing (or a photo gallery) on one platform, screenshots the images, creates a fake account on another platform (or uses a different name on the same platform), and re-lists the item at a competitive price. Unsuspecting buyers see a great deal, pay, and receive nothing.

The scammer's advantage: most buyers shop on one platform at a time. They see the listing, like the price, and buy without cross-checking whether the same item appears elsewhere.

## How Image Theft Scams Work

**Step 1: The scammer finds a desirable item.** Usually, something high-demand and expensive: a vintage Hermès bag, a Chanel flap, a Louis Vuitton trunk. The more desirable the item, the more likely someone will buy without extreme verification. They screenshot the photos from the original listing or find a high-res gallery online.

**Step 2: The scammer creates a fake account.** On Depop, Vinted, or eBay, they set up a new seller profile, sometimes using a real-sounding name, sometimes a generic one. Reviews and account history are blank or deliberately thin (to avoid suspicion of a new account, some scammers use old, dormant accounts they've bought from other scammers).

**Step 3: The scammer lists the item.** Using the stolen photos, they create a listing with a price at or slightly below market rate. Not too cheap (which would trigger suspicion), just cheap enough to look like a deal compared to the original listing. They fill in a description that is either vague (copied from the original) or convincing (tailored to the photos, showing knowledge of the item).

**Step 4: The scammer waits for a buyer.** When someone buys, the scammer collects payment. At this point, they have a choice: they might never ship, or they might ship a counterfeit or a completely different item. Either way, the buyer eventually files a dispute.

**Step 5: The scammer abandons the account.** When disputes start, the scammer has already moved on to a new account, new photos, new items. The account that took the payment becomes inactive.

## The Red Flags

**1. Photos that look too professional or stock-like.** Real sellers photograph items in their homes. You see background clutter, natural light inconsistencies, maybe a hand holding the item. A listing where every photo is perfectly lit, perfectly composed, with a blank or seamless background, is suspicious. It looks like a product listing or a catalog shoot, not someone's closet.

**2. No follow-up photos when asked.** A real seller with the item in hand can provide additional photos on request, a close-up of the interior tag, the serial number, the maker's mark. A scammer using stolen photos cannot. They will be evasive, claim the item is packed already, or simply stop responding.

**3. Reluctance to answer specific questions.** Ask about the weight of the bag, the exact measurements, whether the hardware is tarnished, what condition the lining is in. A real seller who owns the item can answer these immediately. A scammer will give vague answers or repeat the description.

**4. A newly created account with an expensive item.** A brand-new seller with a detailed photo gallery of a $3,000 vintage bag is more likely to be a scammer than a legitimate seller. Most real sellers build a history selling lower-value items first. A new account jumping straight to luxury items is unusual.

**5. The same exact photos on multiple platforms under different sellers.** This is the smoking gun. Use reverse image search (Google Images, TinEye) to check if the photos appear anywhere else. If the same photo appears on Depop under seller "VintageEmporium99" and on Vinted under "LuxeVintage2024," one of them is a scammer using stolen images.

## The Cross-Platform Check: How to Spot Image Duplication

Before you buy a high-value vintage item:

1. **Take a screenshot of the listing photo.** Right-click on one of the main product photos and select "Save Image" or use a reverse image search tool.

2. **Search Google Images with that photo.** Drag the image to the Google Images search box (images.google.com). It will show every page where that exact image appears.

3. **Check for the item across other platforms.** Search the item name and key details (brand, condition, approximate price) on Depop, Vinted, eBay, Poshmark, and TheRealReal. If the same item (same color, same condition description) appears on multiple platforms with the same photos but different sellers, you have caught a scam.

4. **Check the seller's history on each platform.** Click on the seller's profile and look at their other listings. A real seller with multiple items will have a pattern, similar quality, similar categories, consistent photography style. A scammer will have random items at random price points, or no history at all.

5. **Cross-reference the listing details.** Does the description match across platforms? If "Mint condition, unused" on Depop and "Worn twice" on Vinted, and they are using the same photos, that is a scammer covering their tracks poorly.

## Prevention: What to Do Before You Pay

**For items under $100:** The risk is lower. Still check seller history and reviews, but a high-quality photo is usually sufficient.

**For items $100-$500:** Always ask for additional photos (interior tag, maker's mark, wear spots). Use platform-backed payment (Goods & Services on PayPal, Depop's payment protection, eBay's system). Do the reverse image search.

**For items over $500:** Reverse image search is mandatory. Request a video walkthrough of the item. Use an authentication service before payment clears (many high-end platforms offer this). Consider buying only from sellers with extensive, verified history.

**Never wire money.** Wire transfers (Venmo, Western Union, international bank transfer) have no dispute resolution. If you are asked to wire money for a secondhand item, you can assume it is a scam.

## What Platform Protection Actually Covers

| Platform | Buyer protection | What it covers | What it doesn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depop | Depop Direct payment | Item not received; significantly not as described | Seller claims item was shipped but buyer "lost it" |
| Vinted | Platform-held payment | Item not received; counterfeit verification (new) | Subtle misrepresentation if item technically "arrived" |
| eBay | Money Back Guarantee | Item not received; significantly not as described; authenticity guarantee (paid items) | Buyer's remorse; subtle damage if item "as described" claims match |
| Poshmark | Posh Protect | Item not received; significantly different condition | Buyer regret on sizing or color perception |
| TheRealReal | Authenticity guarantee + return period | Counterfeit detection; returns within 7 days | Damage from buyer use |

All platforms offer *some* protection, but they work better when you:
1. Document everything (screenshots of listings, descriptions, seller history)
2. File disputes promptly (timelines range from 30-180 days post-purchase)
3. Communicate with the seller through the platform (not via DM), so there is a record

## The Nuclear Option: Cross-Platform Search Before Buying

Rather than trusting one listing, search the item across all platforms at once. If you are looking for a specific vintage Hermès bag, check what that exact bag (same color, same year, same condition) sells for on:

- Depop
- Vinted  
- eBay
- Poshmark
- TheRealReal
- Vestiaire Collective

If you see wildly different prices (same item, one at $800, one at $1,200), the $800 listing is either a steal or a scam. Cross-check the seller history and photos. If the photos are identical across both listings but the sellers have different names, you have found a scammer.
