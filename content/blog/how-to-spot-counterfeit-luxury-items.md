**Short answer:** Counterfeit luxury items today are good enough that no single visual check is reliable, so the safer approach is a layered verification habit: seller history, photo quality, price sanity, and willingness to provide more documentation before you pay.

A few years ago, spotting a fake often came down to one or two obvious tells, a misspelled logo, an obviously wrong font. Counterfeiters have closed most of those gaps. The checklist approach still helps, but it's no longer sufficient on its own.

## Why brand-specific checklists are losing reliability

Counterfeit production has specialized. The operations making convincing fakes of high-demand brands study the exact stitching patterns, hardware weight, and date code formatting that authentication guides publish, then correct for them in the next production run. A checklist that was accurate a year ago can be outdated against the current generation of fakes.

This doesn't mean visual checks are useless, it means they should be one layer of verification, not the only one. For brand-specific physical details, our guide on [how to avoid super fakes when buying designer secondhand](/blog/how-to-avoid-super-fakes-on-resale-apps) covers what to look for on specific high-risk categories. This post focuses on the layer above that: the buyer-side habits that catch problems regardless of which brand is involved.

## The verification layers that actually hold up

1. **Check the seller's history before the item.** A seller with a real, aged account and a pattern of similar sales is a meaningfully different risk than a brand-new account with one listing of an expensive item. Counterfeit sellers churn through accounts.
2. **Request photos the listing doesn't already have.** Ask for a close-up of the interior tag, a specific hardware stamp, or the stitching at a particular seam. A legitimate seller with the real item in hand can produce this in minutes. A reluctant or evasive response is itself information.
3. **Treat an unusually low price as a question, not a deal.** If an item is priced 40% or more below comparable sold listings for the same condition, that gap needs an explanation before it's a reason to buy faster.
4. **Use paid authentication services for expensive purchases.** For items where the loss would be significant, a third-party authentication service before finalizing the purchase is inexpensive relative to the risk. Several resale platforms now offer this built in.
5. **Understand the platform's actual recourse.** Some platforms verify before shipping, some only offer buyer protection after a dispute is filed. Know which one you're using before you need it, not after.

## A quick reference

| Layer | What it catches |
|---|---|
| Seller history | New or pattern-matched counterfeit accounts |
| Extra photo requests | Sellers without the real item in hand |
| Price sanity check | Listings priced to move fast before scrutiny |
| Paid authentication | Sophisticated fakes that pass visual inspection |
| Platform recourse | Your actual options if something goes wrong anyway |

No single layer catches everything. Together, they catch most of what a checklist alone misses, because counterfeiters who've solved the visual details haven't necessarily solved the seller-behavior and pricing signals.
