**Short answer:** Most resale platforms' authenticity guarantees rely on a human authenticator visually inspecting an item for 60 to 120 seconds, checking stitching, hardware, and tags against a database, but the process often misses super fakes and can flag genuine vintage pieces with replaced parts as suspect. Understanding exactly what these processes screen for, and what they miss, is essential knowledge for anyone buying luxury goods on the secondhand market.

Vestiaire Collective's authenticators are trained to spot a fake Gucci Marmont bag within 90 seconds. They are not trained to evaluate a 1972 Hermes Kelly with a replaced closure buckle and an owner-hand-stitched interior repair. The first task they perform well. The second task causes problems on both sides: super fakes designed to exploit the visual inspection window pass, while genuine vintage pieces with legitimate repairs and alterations fail.

## The Hierarchy of Authenticators

Not all platform authentication is equivalent. The staffing model behind the guarantee determines what you actually get.

**In-house specialist teams** with deep category expertise represent the highest tier of platform authentication. A specialist who has handled thousands of Chanel flap bags across multiple decades can identify subtle variations in chain weight, lambskin texture, and CC lock alignment that a generalist misses entirely. The RealReal employs specialists across categories, and its authentication output is generally considered more reliable for contemporary luxury goods than for vintage pieces from before the 1990s, where the reference database is less complete.

**Part-time trained staff** working from visual checklists represent the majority of authentication capacity on high-volume platforms. These authenticators are trained to identify common counterfeits of specific popular items: the current-season Chanel, the popular LV monogram bag, the Gucci GG canvas tote. They are less equipped to handle vintage pieces, rare models, or items where the counterfeiting sophistication is high enough to pass visual inspection.

**Machine-learning assisted visual screening** supplements human review at most major platforms. Vestiaire Collective has invested in algorithms that flag items during the digital submission phase, before physical inspection. This catches obviously low-quality fakes and clears obviously authentic recent-season pieces, theoretically allowing human specialists to focus their time on genuinely uncertain cases.

## What Triggers a False Negative on Vintage Items

The authentication failure modes that affect genuine vintage pieces are consistent across platforms.

**Replaced hardware.** Luxury bags from the 1970s and 1980s that have been in active use for decades often have replaced closures, buckles, feet, or chains. The original owner had the work done by a reputable leather goods repair shop, which used hardware that was correct for the period but sourced from non-original supplier stock. An authenticator checking the hardware against a reference database for a contemporary equivalent will register a mismatch, flag the piece, and it fails authentication. The bag is genuine; the hardware swap happened legitimately thirty years ago.

**Owner alterations.** High-end garments were altered by their original owners far more commonly in earlier decades than today. A 1960s Balenciaga dress shortened by a skilled tailor in 1975, or a 1980s Chanel jacket with a replaced button in the correct style but not the original manufacturer, can fail modern authentication not because it is a fake but because it has been altered by someone who owned it.

**Irregular vintage stitching.** Luxury goods from earlier production periods were often made with less machine precision than contemporary pieces. An authenticator calibrated to the even, regular stitching of contemporary production may flag the slightly irregular handwork of a genuine 1970s Hermes as suspicious.

**Worn or faded branding.** Genuine 40-year-old pieces have genuine 40-year-old patina. Brand stamps fade. Serial number plaques wear. Lining text becomes difficult to read. An authenticator's reference database is populated primarily with contemporary or near-contemporary examples where these elements are crisp, creating a situation where age itself becomes an authentication risk.

## How Super Fakes Exploit the 90-Second Window

The counterfeit production ecosystem has industrialised to a degree that most buyers do not appreciate. The term 'super fake' refers to counterfeits produced with genuine high-quality hardware, accurate-weight metals, correctly sourced leather, and correct internal construction to a level of precision that passes visual inspection by a trained but time-constrained authenticator.

Super fakes are designed specifically for the authentication window. They pass on the dimensions a 90-second check covers: the weight of the hardware, the appearance of the stitching, the format of the serial number, the texture of the leather on the surfaces that are examined. Where they fail, if examined by a specialist with adequate time and specialist tools, is in areas that are not reached in a standard check: the internal frame construction, the specific density of hardware metal under a jeweller's scale, the micro-texture of the leather under magnification.

The platforms with the best record of catching super fakes are those that combine human expertise with technology tools, primarily weight verification, material analysis, and AI-pattern recognition against databases that include known super-fake variants. These are capabilities that the highest-tier authentication services are developing, but that are not yet standard at the platform level.

## What Happens When You Dispute After Purchase

Every major platform's buyer protection policy covers authenticity disputes, but the process of actually resolving one is more involved than the marketing language suggests.

A successful dispute requires more than a feeling that something is wrong. Platforms typically require one of: a detailed written report from a qualified independent authenticator, a letter from the brand's customer service confirming the item is not authentic, or documentation from a specialist dealer or auction house making the same determination.

Independent authentication services, the specialists who work outside any single platform and can be hired by buyers or sellers, range from single-category experts to multi-category firms. Their reports carry more weight in disputes than any individual's assessment, and for items above approximately $2,000 their fee is usually justified by the protection it provides.
