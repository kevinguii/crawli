**Short answer:** To price secondhand clothing for a fast sale, search the same item's sold listings on your platform, take the median sold price, and list within 10 to 15% of it, then adjust for condition, season, and platform fees. What you originally paid is irrelevant to what a buyer will offer, so set the price from the market, not from your receipt.

Research from Depop's 2022 seller data shows items priced competitively against current market comps sell approximately 30% faster than items priced based on original purchase cost. The psychology is simple: buyers know what things are worth. They've searched the same item. They've seen the sold prices. If your listing is 25% above market, they move on and don't send an offer.

## Price from sold listings, not asking prices

This is the single most common pricing error, and it is worth separating out from everything else.

Active listings tell you what sellers *hope* to get. Sold listings tell you what buyers actually paid. Those two numbers routinely differ by 30% or more, and they differ in a predictable direction: the pool of active listings is biased high, because the overpriced items are precisely the ones that never sold and are still sitting there. If you price against active listings, you are anchoring to a set of failures.

Use asking prices for one thing only: seeing where your listing will land on a search results page next to its competition. Use sold prices to set the number.

## The five factors that actually set price

1. **Brand recognition:** not just prestige, but current relevance. A brand that was hot three years ago but faded moves at a discount now. A brand that's trending up commands a premium even on worn pieces.
2. **Condition:** this is the most subjective factor and where sellers consistently over-rate their own items. "Like new" should mean no signs of wear whatsoever. Most sellers call "good" what buyers call "well-worn." Be more conservative than you think you need to be.
3. **Current demand:** seasonal and trend-driven. A linen blazer in April will move faster and at a higher price than the same blazer in November. Vintage silhouettes that just hit a trend cycle (oversized leather jackets, for example) sell at a premium for 12 to 18 months, then flatten.
4. **Seasonality:** list warm-weather pieces in spring, heavy outerwear in September. Listing out of season is fine if you're patient and price 15 to 20% below market. Buyers do stock up, but they want a discount for the wait.
5. **Platform fees:** these vary widely and come straight off your payout, so price against net proceeds, not gross. Poshmark takes 20% over $15, Mercari 10%, eBay 13.6%, while Depop and Vinted charge sellers little or nothing. See the table below.

## A practical pricing formula

**Step 1:** Search the same item on the platform you're selling on. Filter to sold listings, not active. Take 10 to 15 sold comps, ignore the outliers (the one that sold for $10 and the one that sold for $300), and find the median.

**Step 2:** Adjust for condition. Your item one grade better than average: add 15%. One grade worse: subtract 15%.

**Step 3:** Add fees back in. If Poshmark takes 20% and you want $40 net, list at $50.

**Step 4:** Check seasonality. Off-season: discount 15 to 20% from the median. Peak season: you can push 10% above median.

**Step 5:** Set a floor. The lowest you'll accept should be your break-even (cost plus fees plus shipping if you offer free). Don't list below your floor.

## Pricing vintage clothing is a different problem

Everything above assumes you can find ten comparable sold listings. For genuine vintage, you often cannot, and the factors that set the price change shape.

**Scarcity outranks condition.** On a modern item, wear is a straightforward discount. On vintage, a rare piece in mediocre condition can outsell a common piece in excellent condition by a wide margin. Fading, repairs, and honest wear are frequently priced as character rather than damage, particularly in denim, military surplus, and band merch.

**Era markers carry real money.** Buyers pay for provenance they can verify from photos. The details that move price:

- **Single stitch** hems and sleeves on tees, generally pre mid 1990s
- **Union labels** and "Made in USA" tags, which date a garment to a specific window
- **Deadstock** condition, meaning unworn with original tags, which can multiply the price several times over
- **Copyright dates** on printed graphics, the most reliable dating evidence on band and tour merch
- **Fabric composition** consistent with the claimed era, since a modern blend in a supposedly 1970s garment is a reproduction tell

If you are unsure what you have, work through the [vintage band tee identification guide](/blog/how-to-identify-vintage-band-tee) or the [fabric and construction guides](/blog) before setting a number. Misdating a piece by a decade is usually a larger pricing error than misjudging its condition.

**Widen the comp window.** For a modern item, 30 days of sold listings is plenty. For vintage, thin supply means you may need 6 to 12 months of sold data to gather enough comps to find a defensible median. Fewer than five comps is a guess, so say so to yourself and price conservatively rather than pretending to precision you do not have.

**Watch the reproduction floor.** Heavily reproduced categories (band tees, Carhartt, Harley merch, 1990s sportswear) have a large pool of cheap fakes and modern retro prints sitting in the same search results. If your comp search returns a spread of $20 items and $300 items on what looks like the same design, you are almost certainly looking at two different products, and averaging them produces a meaningless number. Separate them before you take a median.

## The emotional pricing mistake

Most sellers know their items' original prices. That number sticks. A jacket bought for $150 feels like it should sell for at least $80. But the market doesn't care what you paid. If comparable jackets are selling for $45, your floor of $80 just put you in the "never sells" category.

The fix: look up the sold comps before you look at your receipt. Form your price expectation from the market, not from memory.

## What to do with items that won't sell

If an item hasn't sold in four weeks, run this diagnosis:

- **Lots of views, no offers:** overpriced relative to comps. Drop 10 to 15%.
- **Few views:** visibility problem, not price. Relist during peak hours.
- **Likes but no offers:** you're close to the buyer's threshold. Send a small offer to likers (Poshmark makes this easy), often converts within 24 hours.
- **Nothing at all (no views, no likes, no offers):** either very niche or the listing has a photo or title problem. Reshoot with a plain background and rewrite the title with the brand, style name, and condition.
