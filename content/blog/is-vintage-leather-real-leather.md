**Short answer:** Most vintage leather sold as such is real animal hide, but the term "genuine leather" is a grade, not a guarantee, and it can sit right next to bonded leather, which is ground-up leather fibers glued together and coated to look like a solid hide. You can verify real leather without a lighter by checking four things: an irregular natural pore pattern, the smell of animal hide rather than plastic, a fibrous suede-like underside, and the way a tiny water drop slowly absorbs and darkens instead of beading on the surface.

That vintage bomber you almost bought smelled faintly of a new car, not a barn. That is worth pausing on. Real hide has a warm, slightly musty animal smell that a coated synthetic cannot fake, and the seller who priced it like leather may not know the difference. Here is how to test it in person, or from listing photos, without taking a flame to the hem.

## "Genuine Leather" Is a Grade, Not a Compliment

The leather grades you will run into, from best to worst, are roughly full-grain, top-grain, corrected-grain, "genuine" or split, and bonded. Full-grain keeps the whole outer surface of the hide with all its natural marks and is the most durable. "Genuine leather" usually means a lower split of the skin, sanded and finished, still real but nowhere near the top of the range. Bonded leather is the outlier: it is leather scraps ground into a pulp, bonded with glue onto a backing, then coated and embossed with a fake grain. It contains real leather content, so a label can technically call it leather, but it cracks, peels, and flakes much more like a plastic than a hide.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's guides for the leather industry generally require that ground, pulverized, or bonded leather be disclosed as such rather than passed off as solid hide, which is exactly why a vague "genuine leather" stamp with no other detail is a signal to look closer, not a reason to relax.

## The 5 Non-Destructive Tests

None of these mark or ruin the piece, which matters whether you are keeping it or reselling it. Work through them in order. Real leather passes most of them comfortably; a convincing synthetic usually fails at least two.

## Why 1970s and 1980s Leather Often Beats Modern "Leather"

Convincing synthetic leather is largely a modern development. A jacket genuinely from the 1970s or 1980s was overwhelmingly likely to be real hide, often full-grain or top-grain, because the cheap coated alternatives that flood fast fashion today either did not exist yet or looked obviously plastic. That is a big part of why vintage leather holds up: you are frequently buying a better grade of hide than a comparable new mid-market jacket, which is also why [vintage clothing so often holds its value](/blog/why-vintage-clothing-holds-its-value).

## Reading a Vintage Leather Label

Old union labels, country-of-origin tags, and material stamps help, but read them alongside the physical tests rather than instead of them. A stamp reading "genuine leather" or "man-made materials" is informative; a jacket with no material tag at all is common in genuine vintage and is not itself a red flag. What matters is that the label and the hide agree with each other. If the tag says leather and the piece smells like plastic and has a woven back, believe the piece.

If suede is your worry rather than a smooth hide, the tell is different, and the same water-drop logic applies in reverse. That is its own guide: [suede vs split leather and how to spot the fake](/blog/suede-vs-split-leather). ## When the Seller Won't Take Returns

The tests above are your protection when a listing is final sale, which a lot of peer-to-peer vintage is. But there is a second layer of protection: price. If a "leather" jacket is listed for $50 on one platform and the same style, or a verified-real equivalent, sits on a marketplace with buyer protection for $70, the $20 you save on the cheaper listing means nothing if it turns up as coated pleather with no recourse.
