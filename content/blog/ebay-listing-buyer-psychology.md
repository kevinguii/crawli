**Short answer:** eBay listings underperform less often because of weak photos and more often because they leave the buyer's real questions unanswered. Vintage and secondhand buyers are silently asking about fit, cleanliness, authenticity, shipping speed, and return risk while they scroll. A listing that answers all five before the buyer has to ask converts noticeably better than one that just has more photos.

Most eBay advice tells sellers to add keywords and take more pictures. Both help, but they treat the symptom, not the cause. A buyer scrolling past a vintage listing is running a fast, mostly unconscious risk check, and the listings that lose them are the ones that leave that check unanswered.

## The five silent questions every vintage buyer asks

Nobody types these into the message box. They just scroll past if the listing doesn't answer them.

1. **Will it fit?** A size label alone doesn't answer this for vintage. A "size 8" dress from the 1970s and a "size 8" dress from 2015 are different garments. Buyers want measurements, not a label they have to mentally translate.
2. **Is it clean?** Secondhand carries a built-in hesitation about smell, staining, and general wear that new items don't. Silence on this point reads as something to hide, even when there's nothing wrong with the item.
3. **Is it real?** Anything with brand value invites a quiet authenticity check. Buyers look for specific, checkable details, not just the word "authentic" repeated in the title.
4. **How fast will it ship?** Buyers comparing several listings for the same item often default to whichever one removes the most uncertainty, and handling time is one of the easiest places to do that.
5. **Can I return it?** This is the question that decides whether a hesitant buyer clicks buy or closes the tab. No visible return policy reads as more risk than a bad one.

## Measurements in inches aren't optional for vintage

Vintage and secondhand sizing has never been standardized, and it shifts across decades, regions, and brands. A modern size medium and a 1990s size medium can differ by multiple inches at the chest or waist. Sellers who list only the tag size are asking the buyer to gamble.

The fix is mechanical: pit-to-pit, shoulder to hem, waist, and inseam, laid flat in inches, in the same order every time. Buyers comparing several listings gravitate toward the one that lets them check against a piece they already own. That's the detail that prevents the size-related return that costs you both the sale and the shipping.

## Signaling care without sounding like boilerplate

"Smoke-free, pet-free home" has been copy-pasted into so many listings that it's started to read as noise. The fix isn't dropping the claim, it's making it specific and verifiable in tone. "Steamed before listing, stored in a smoke-free closet" reads differently than a generic disclaimer, because it describes an action the buyer can picture, not a policy statement. Specificity is what separates a trust signal from filler text, and buyers can tell the difference even if they can't articulate why.

## Using item specifics correctly, not just completely

Item specifics, the structured fields for brand, era, material, size, and category-specific details, do more work than most sellers realize. They feed eBay's Cassini search system for relevance matching, and they power the filters buyers use to narrow results. A buyer filtering by decade or material will never see a listing that left those fields blank, no matter how strong the title and photos are. Filling in decade, designer, and era isn't busywork, it's what makes the listing eligible to show up in the searches buyers are actually running.

## Why a clear return policy is a conversion tool

Sellers tend to think about returns purely as a cost, something that happens after the sale when something goes wrong. But the return policy does its real work before the sale, at the exact moment a hesitant buyer is deciding whether the risk is worth it. A visible, specific return window (eBay's own guidance recommends 30 days as a default for most categories) removes the single biggest objection a buyer has when they can't try something on first. Offering returns isn't giving something away. It's removing the reason a buyer was about to close the tab.

## How trust signals differ across platforms

The mechanism is the same everywhere: buyers look for evidence before they commit, but the structure for delivering it isn't. Some marketplaces rely heavily on seller reputation and informal back-and-forth messaging to resolve uncertainty, which puts the burden on the buyer to ask and the seller to respond fast enough. eBay's structured fields, return-policy controls, and case system mean a lot of that reassurance can be built into the listing itself, before a buyer ever has to send a message. A listing that uses item specifics, measurements, and a visible return policy does the reassurance work upfront instead of leaving it to a conversation that might not happen before the buyer moves on.

## Putting it together

Go back through your active listings and check each one against the five questions above. Add inch measurements if they're missing. Replace generic care language with one specific, concrete detail. Fill in every item specific field your category offers, not just the required ones. Make your return policy visible, not buried. None of this requires new inventory or new photos, just a listing that does more of the buyer's thinking for them.
