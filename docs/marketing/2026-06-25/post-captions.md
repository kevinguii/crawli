```

```
You can often guess a vintage garment's decade before you ever check the tag, just from how it hangs on the hanger. The shoulder line and the way the hem falls give it away.

1930s pieces drape on a diagonal. Bias cut dresses pull at an angle, so if you lift the hem it pools toward one side instead of hanging straight.

## Post 2 — The Union Label Date Stamp (Vintage Community)

**TikTok Title**
```
The union label inside vintage clothing is a free date stamp
```

**TikTok Description**
```
That small union label stitched inside a vintage dress is basically a free date stamp, if you know how to read it. The ILGWU label changed design across the decades, and each version pins down an era.

Before 1955 the label was a red, white and blue shield with an eagle inside the outline. It is the oldest of the common union labels you will find in American vintage.

From 1955 to 1963 the shield gave way to a blue and white oval that read Union Made. If you see the oval, you are likely in the late 1950s or very early 1960s.

In the late 1960s through the 1970s the label shrank into a small woven tag, usually red lettering on white, much plainer than the earlier versions.

By the 1980s it became a simple rectangle with a tiny circular logo. That was the last common format before the ILGWU merged in 1995 and the label disappeared.

That 1995 merger is your hard cutoff. A genuine ILGWU label means the piece predates it, which already tells you a lot about age and origin.

## Post 4 — Boro and Sashiko (Vintage Community)

**TikTok Title**
```
Boro and sashiko started as survival mending in rural Japan
```

**TikTok Description**
```
Boro and sashiko look like a refined craft aesthetic now, but they began as a survival method in 19th century rural Japan, born from a culture that treated fabric as far too precious to waste.

Boro literally means rags. During the Edo and Meiji periods, families who could not afford new cloth layered and stitched scraps of indigo cotton over and over, building thick, warm garments that were never thrown away.

Sashiko is the running stitch that held it all together. It was used to reinforce and patch worn fabric, and its geometric patterns started as a practical side effect before becoming an art form in their own right.

Indigo tells the truth on a real piece. Authentic boro fades unevenly with age and often leaves indigo dye transfer on the reverse, something a brand new reproduction will not show.

Read the repairs. A genuine antique boro piece carries many generations of hand mending stacked on top of each other, not one clean machine pass meant to look old.

True antique boro is rare and expensive, so when you are hunting it, scanning a lot of listings across many platforms is the only way to actually catch one.

## Post 5 — A Flawless Bag's Secret (Fashionistas)

**TikTok Title**
```
A vintage bag that looks flawless was probably repainted
```

**TikTok Description**
```
Many vintage handbags sold as mint condition have actually been professionally repainted, and that retouching is hidden in places most listings never photograph. Here are four signs that separate a repainted bag from an untouched one.

Check the stitch holes first. Repainting leaves color buildup sitting inside the stitch holes and on the thread itself, because spray paint does not know to avoid them.

Look at the edges. Refinished edges read slightly rounded and waxy, with a finish that looks too uniform and even to be the original factory work.

Compare hidden to exposed. The area under the flap or inside a pocket is usually left untouched, so match that protected color against the exposed exterior. A real difference is a tell.

Trust your nose. A repainted bag often carries a faint acrylic or solvent smell, something genuine aged leather simply does not have.

A repainted bag is not always a bad buy, but it should be priced below an unaltered piece with natural patina, because honest leather develops character while paint just peels.

## Post 7 — It May Have Walked a Runway (Fashionistas)

**TikTok Title**
```
A jacket with a sharpie number inside may be a runway sample
```

**TikTok Description**
```
That jacket you found with a missing size tag and a sharpie marking inside might have walked a runway. A lot of garments floating around secondhand are actually runway and showroom samples, not production pieces, and they leave clues.

Runway samples are the exact garments made for shows, press and showroom appointments. They are usually produced in a single standard size, sometimes with construction that differs slightly from the final retail version.

Look for a sharpie number. Samples are tracked by hand inside the brand, so a number written in marker inside the garment is a strong tell.

A missing care or size tag. Press and showroom pieces often skip the consumer labels entirely, so a garment with no size tag next to a normal care label is worth a second look.

One single sample size. Brands build their samples on one fit, usually a 38 or a US 4 to 6, so a piece in that size with no size tag fits the pattern.

Finer made, but more fragile. Sample pieces can be more carefully constructed than retail, yet they were never built to survive years of real wear.

A missing tag is not always damage. Sometimes it is provenance, a small piece of the brand's internal story. Crawli searches every platform at once, so you can chase those sample clues wherever they surface. thecrawli.com
